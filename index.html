About the data and design

This visualization uses the supplied NOAA daily weather dataset for 2017, which covers January 1 to September 21. Each location is a weather station in a U.S. state or territory, and we excluded the Canadian stations. Because average temperature was often missing, we used the daily midpoint temperature, calculated as (Minimum Temperature + Maximum Temperature) / 2. Our question is which locations have the most stable temperatures, and whether the answer changes by season.

Stability is measured by the standard deviation of daily midpoint temperatures, where a lower value means temperatures stayed closer to their period average. Stations need observations for at least 80% of the available dates in the selected period so that poorly covered stations cannot rank highly by accident. Winter (January to February) and fall (September 1 to 21) are only partial seasons, and a single year does not describe long-term climate.

We used horizontal bars because the main task is ranking and comparing stations, and bar length is an accurate encoding that leaves room for long station names. by using a bar, viewers can be able to quickly compare different temperatures from different stations just by viewing the graph. Every bar starts at zero and the scale stays the same across seasons, so changes in bar length reflect real changes in the data. The interactions are a season dropdown that works as a dynamic filter, and tooltips that provide details on you hover or click on a bar. Bars also get a highlight outline on hover and can be focused with the keyboard. When the season changes, bars animate to their new rank and width and entering or leaving stations fade, so users can follow a station from one season to the next. Some alternate designs that we considered were a map, a calendar heatmap, and a daily line chart, but we chose not to use those designs as it hid the ranking or showed too much at once, so we removed them to keep the visualization focused on one question.

Our main challenges were presenting standard deviation in a way that is easy to understand, handling missing average temperatures, and making the tooltip feel natural. Our first tooltip appeared in the same spot no matter where the cursor was, so we changed it to follow the cursor and stay on screen. We also spent time making the season transitions smooth by keying each bar to a specific station.

Source: CMSC 471 Assignment 1, supplied weather.csv (NOAA daily weather observations). The code structure, transitions, and tooltip interaction draw on our Lab 4 D3 visualization. The chart is implemented with D3.js (version 7.9.0).

Development process

We split the work evenly. Matthew handled data preparation, including filtering out the Canadian stations, computing the daily midpoint temperatures, and calculating the standard deviation and 80% coverage rule for each season. Jonathon wrote the page layout and styling, the write-up, and the accessibility features such as keyboard focus and labels. Joshua built the D3 chart, the season transitions, and the cursor-following tooltip, and recorded the demo video. We all tested the page and reviewed each other's code. We spent roughly 5 people-hours in total, and the data preparation and the tooltip and transitions took the most time.
